THE IDEA BEING TAUGHT
 
{goal}:{goal_description}
 
WHAT MAKES THIS METHOD WORK
 
This method doesn't lecture and then quiz. It runs a loop: pose a small case → the reader commits to an answer in their head → reveal the answer → the reveal confirms or gently surprises → the next case builds on what just clicked. The reader discovers each principle a beat before it's named, so naming it feels like recognition, not instruction. Your entire job is to recreate that loop.
 
The single most important property: MANY SHORT EXCHANGES, NOT FEW LONG ONES. A dozen quick questions beat three thick ones. Each step moves one small inch. If you feel a question carrying two ideas, split it into two questions.
 
STEP 1 — DIAGNOSE THE MATERIAL
 
Classify before writing, because the method adapts to the subject's shape:
 
- DERIVATIONAL (math, logic, programming, parts of physics/econ/grammar): concepts compose. → Original method applies directly; the reader reasons out most answers from prior steps.
- FACTUAL/FLAT (anatomy, vocabulary, taxonomy, dates): many items must be told. → Use the loop for relationships and organizing principles; isolate irreducible facts as "(given)" questions. Don't fake derivations.
- INTERPRETIVE (history, literature, ethics, law): answers are judgments. → Questions lead the reader to weigh evidence and notice tensions. Keep answers short even here — surface ONE consideration per question rather than a paragraph of analysis.
 
Most material is a mix. State your read in one line before starting.
 
STEP 2 — STRUCTURE
 
Identify the 3-7 sub-ideas that build toward the target idea, in dependency order, and the simplest concrete instance of each. Plan for many tiny steps.
 
STEP 3 — WRITE THE DIALOGUE
 
A short SCENARIO PARAGRAPH before a section is encouraged — set up the concrete situation, characters, or objects the questions will use, so the questions themselves can stay tiny. Set the scene once, then ask many small questions against it.
 
Then obey these principles:
 
1. NEVER state a principle before the reader can meet it through a case. Show instances; let the rule surface; name it only once it's effectively understood.
2. Each question is answerable from what came before, yet not trivial. That small gap is the method.
3. KEEP BOTH QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS SHORT. Most answers are a word, a number, or one short clause — "Yes," "No," "Smaller," "Because it doubles." A question is one sentence. If an answer needs more than two short sentences, you're skipping steps — break it into more questions instead.
4. Concrete before abstract, always.
5. Make answers feel earned — the reader confirming their own reasoning ("Yes — it shrinks"), not receiving a lecture.
6. Repeat structural patterns with fresh content to cement them.
7. Name the formal term only after the idea is familiar from use.
8. When a durable, reusable principle emerges, crystallize it as a short memorable rule the reader carries forward (an “Insight"). Restate it verbatim when it recurs.
9. Warm, light tone. Use a recurring concrete motif only if neutral placeholder objects genuinely help the subject; otherwise skip it.
10. Fold recaps into the dialogue as one small question that makes the reader assemble the whole idea — not an appended quiz.
11. Each Q&A pair must be a necessary step in a single chain of reasoning 
toward the goal endpoint. Ask yourself: if this pair were removed, would 
the student be unable to reach the next idea? If no — cut it. Do not 
cover related ideas, adjacent concepts, or material outside this goal.
   
SELF-CHECK BEFORE FINISHING
 
Confirm: (a) you never explained a concept before the reader could meet it; (b) every question has a real but crossable gap; (c) no answer runs long where two questions would serve better; (d) the reader actually arrives at THE IDEA BEING TAUGHT by the end. Rewrite anything that fails.
